Growth investing & due diligence with IRIS.

Detailed notes

With Nicolas Herschtel

Partner at IRIS, growth investor in software & technology and board member.

Executive summary · 2 min 14 questions & answers
Executive summary2-minute read

Session 5 in six points.

  1. Venture takes a company from zero to about €10M of revenue: finding product-market fit and a go-to-market. Growth takes a proven engine and scales it, with less risk.
  2. Growth rounds mix primary (new money) and secondary (buying out earlier investors), because VCs increasingly need to return cash to their own LPs.
  3. Growth due diligence takes 3–6 months and is very analytical: cohorts, NRR by segment, many customer calls. IRIS does its commercial diligence in-house.
  4. His three KPIs: net revenue retention, the cost of acquiring a customer, and operating costs. His big red flag: churn hidden by fast sales.
  5. The 2020–2022 bubble left many companies valued too high to exit. Discipline on valuation, and on terms like liquidation preferences, pays off over time.
  6. Exits: IPOs are rare and trade sales hard to predict, so the main route is selling to private equity. His job is to make companies "PE compatible".

01The speaker & IRIS

From real estate to software.

Nicolas Herschtel didn't plan a career in tech. He says himself he is "not a big fan of tech", and that it is "totally by surprise" that he ended up there.

Nicolas Herschtel, Partner

1
Business school, then M&A

He started as an M&A adviser at a bank. One of his clients was a real-estate company.

2
Real-estate investing

He crossed to the investor side, with that client's group (Batipart / Covivio), and learned to invest.

3
FSI, around 2008

Joined France's strategic investment fund near its creation. Started investing as a minority shareholder across sectors, which is where software began.

4
Bpifrance Large Venture, 2013

Joined the team building Bpifrance's growth fund, which he described as one of the first growth funds in France, maybe Europe. Seven years of growth investing, with board seats at Doctolib (from 2017), Contentsquare, Kyriba, Talend, Payfit and Botify.

5
InfraVia, then IRIS

Partner at InfraVia from 2020, where he launched its growth activity in Europe (and sat on Botify's board). Joined IRIS as Partner in September 2022 to lead growth investing in France. Today on the board of Implicity, which IRIS backed in December 2025.

Real estate taught him to value predictability. Software is intangible, but with recurring revenue and NRR the future cash flows are quite visible. He sees that as his edge: a clear read on the business plan, without getting carried away by the technology.

02Venture vs growth

Find the fit, then scale it.

His framework is for software and SaaS companies, and he made it simple: the stage is defined by revenue, not by the letter of the round.

€0–10M

Venture

Find product-market fit: test an idea with a few customers, adapt the product until it fits. Then find a go-to-market that works, and bring in the first C-level hires. Lots of risk on the product, the market and the sales. Around €1M of revenue, you start proving the product fits.

€10M+

Growth

The engine is proven; now scale it. Hire, open countries, maybe acquire. There is still risk, but less of it. In letters, Series B sits between venture and growth, and Series C is growth.

Don't trust the letters

Some companies raise every year and burn through letters fast. Others raise big once and wait two years. Some bootstrap to €10–20M of revenue on customers' money, then raise for the first time: technically a "Series A", but a growth deal. What matters is revenue, and even headcount, though AI is making headcount a weaker signal.

03Primary & secondary

New money, or someone's exit.

Primary

Cash into the company

New shares to recruit and scale. Venture is primary only.

Secondary

Buying existing shares

Buying shares from earlier investors, usually VCs. Growth rounds increasingly mix the two.

Why more secondaries? Many VCs need to return money to their own LPs, so they look for exits, and growth rounds give them one.

What changed since the bubble

1
Then: raise a lot

When valuations doubled, the same 10% stake raised twice the money (€30M instead of €15M). Companies took far more primary than they needed.

2
Then: grow at any cost

Investors only looked at the top line. Burning twice as much was fine if growth followed, so a lot of growth was inefficient.

3
Now: efficiency

Lower valuations mean raising less. Companies try to be capital-efficient and get closer to profitability, so growth-stage cash injections are smaller.

04Venture and growth under one roof

Why IRIS does both.

1
The network

Most growth deals are VC-backed, so sourcing means knowing the VC ecosystem. Colleagues on the venture side know the founders and funds, which opens doors.

2
An early view on AI

The venture team meets the companies building what could disrupt software. Despite the "SaaSpocalypse" fears, he sees AI more as an opportunity than a threat for software, depending on the company.

3
Sector expertise

Shared technical and sector knowledge across both teams.

4
History with companies

The venture team may have met a company three or four years before the growth round. The founders know IRIS, and IRIS has seen how the company evolved.

05The growth market

A young market, still digesting a bubble.

Growth investing has existed in the US for decades. In Europe it only really started around 2010, and boomed after Covid, in 2020–2022.

Many ways to do growth

Minority growth

Approach

A minority stake alongside founders and earlier investors.

Who

What IRIS does.

Hypergrowth only

Approach

Only companies growing 50%+ a year.

Who

Funds betting on the fastest companies.

Value-oriented

Approach

Invest at a lower valuation, closer to private equity.

Who

Funds sitting between VC and buyout.

"I would not say one is better than the other." Growth sits between VC and private equity, and definitions vary a lot from one fund to another.

The hangover

During the boom, many new growth funds, often run by former VCs, invested at very high valuations. Today, investors push those companies back toward profitability to avoid raising again at a lower price. Many can't exit: their last valuation is too high for buyers, and earlier investors don't want to sell at a discount. That is a big reason exits are scarce. IRIS stayed disciplined, was less active in 2021–2022, and can now exit at fair market value.

06After the deal

Anticipate the next organisation.

A company at €100M of revenue doesn't work like one at €20M. The growth investor's value is seeing what has to change before it breaks.

  • People. Someone excellent for a company of 20–50 may not be the person for 500. Sometimes that includes the CEO.
  • Structure. Hiring directors, setting up new layers of management, preparing international expansion.
  • Timing. Anticipating means the company can make changes on time, not in a crisis.

He calls IRIS "quite involved", maybe more than VCs, while admitting he isn't a VC, so he can't be sure.

07Due diligence

Three to six months, very analytical.

A growth deal takes longer than a VC round because there is much more data. Companies are usually above €10M of revenue, so diligence goes deep into the numbers: cohorts, NRR by customer segment, and many customer calls.

Bankers run most deals now

Five years ago, many founders raised without a banker. Today most growth deals are intermediated. The banker's job is to sell the company, so the figures are presented in the best light. The investor's job is to cross-check everything.

Buyout funds vs IRIS

Buyout / PE

Phase 1: strategic advisers produce the commercial diligence (market, competitors, potential). Phase 2: the 2–3 shortlisted bidders spend heavily on full due diligence. A lot of money, sometimes for nothing if they lose.

IRIS

Rarely uses external commercial advisers. Does the commercial diligence itself, before the term sheet: calling customers, competitors and experts, often through expert networks. Then issues a term sheet.

After the term sheet: confirmatory diligence

1
Legal

Lawyers check labour, tax, IP and corporate law.

2
Financial

Checks that revenue and figures are what they claim to be. Buyout funds go further, with a full review of the business plan, because they must report to the banks financing their debt. IRIS uses no leverage, so it doesn't need all that.

3
Technical

Experts assess the quality of the product and the code.

VCs usually do lighter diligence, mostly legal, because there is less data and less to check.

08The KPIs that matter

Three numbers he always checks.

1 · Net revenue retention

a
Logo churn

Start the year with 100 customers, end with 95: you lost 5 logos.

b
Gross revenue retention (GRR)

Often the lost customers were the smallest, so revenue retention is higher than logo retention: around 92–95%.

c
Expansion

Upsell (more seats, more countries) and cross-sell (new products, common in enterprise software) can more than make up for churn.

d
NRR

Churn plus expansion. With a strong NRR the company grows, say 10% a year, without a single new customer. NRR also shows what growth is sustainable without spending on acquisition.

2 · Customer acquisition cost

What it costs to win a new customer, and how efficient sales and marketing are. If new customers are cheap to acquire, you should invest more in sales and marketing. Look deeper too: some customers need so much support that they cost far more than they seem.

3 · Operating costs

Beyond sales and marketing: R&D, which is getting lower thanks to AI, and G&A, which rises if the company is international with subsidiaries in several countries.

His biggest red flag: hidden churn

High churn can be masked by high sales velocity. Companies that are easy to sell to are often easy to leave. The top line grows nicely, but churn is revenue at risk, and at some point the sales machine can't keep compensating. SMB customers churn more than enterprise ones, so he prefers enterprise software. For an SMB business, check that the LTV/CAC ratio proves the model works, accounting for margins and churn.

09Terms & structuring

Protect the downside, keep the relationship.

1x

Non-participating preference

The normal growth term. If the company is sold for less, the investor gets its money back first, even if the valuation went down. Preferences matter more at late stage, where tickets are bigger.

2x+

Participating, multiples

A warning sign. If a company accepts it, either the valuation is wrong or the founders are desperate. After the bubble, some companies accepted a high headline valuation with 2x participating preferences to compensate.

Rather than pushing preference multiples, he prefers creative structures: a mix of ordinary and preferred shares, or a minimum return such as a 7–8% coupon, to satisfy every stakeholder.

Win once, or win againAggressive terms crush earlier investors' waterfall. In Europe, VCs bring growth funds their deal flow: "you will win once, but you will not win the second time." The trust people place in you counts for more than a single deal.

Founders' stake

By growth stage, after several rounds at around 20% dilution each, founders may no longer hold a majority. What matters is that management keeps enough interest. If founders are down to around 10%, a new incentive plan can realign them.

10Exits

The main route: private equity.

1
IPO

When he started growth investing, every exit plan said IPO. In practice it is rare: it depends on the market being open when you want to go, there is a queue, European markets are weaker than the US, and listing in the US means being strong there. A possibility, never the investment case.

2
Trade sale

Worth mapping potential buyers when investing, but over four or five years a corporate's management or strategy can change. You can't rely on it.

3
Private equity (main route)

Many companies want to internationalise or acquire, and PE can fund that. His role is to help them become "PE compatible".

4
Secondary sale

If the exit timing is missed, the fund may sell its stake to another investor, often at a discount.

Valuation discipline

A growth fund holds 12–15 companies and can't assume all of them become €1B+ companies. He tends to invest at lower valuations, accepting a higher price only for the rare company he is convinced can reach that scale.

11 · Q&A with the Baby VC batch #14 fellows14 questions

Questions & answers.

If you don't use advisers, how many processes can you run at once?

Team

Nicolas Herschtel It depends on the team. He used to work as one partner and one associate; now the team has a partner, director, principal and associate. Roughly two to three deals at once, and they're rarely all at the most intense stage at the same moment.

Which three KPIs do you absolutely look at?

Metrics

Nicolas Herschtel NRR first (with gross retention), then the cost of acquiring new customers, then operating costs such as R&D and G&A. See chapter 08.

What's your biggest red flag?

Due diligence

Nicolas Herschtel High churn compensated by easy sales. Easy to sell often means easy to churn. The top line looks good, but at some point the sales machine can't keep up. That's why he prefers enterprise software to SMB.

Is there a minimum founder ownership you look for on the cap table?

Cap table

Nicolas Herschtel It depends on the number of founders and on whether the company is VC-backed. After three rounds at about 20% dilution, it's normal for founders to have lost the majority. What matters is enough interest: if they're down to around 10%, a new incentive plan can fix it.

Why would a company you're talking to send a teaser?

Process

Nicolas Herschtel In a banker-led process, everything is prepared: teaser, NDA, data room, Q&A. Without a banker it's less formal: a call, a few slides, and if interested, access to the data.

How do you source, and how many deals do you see?

Sourcing

Nicolas Herschtel He talks to VCs regularly to cover what's coming in their portfolios. A VC will tell him about a company that may be raising, introduce him, and he follows it. He doesn't count how many deals he sees.

Do VC and growth teams need technical profiles?

Careers

Nicolas Herschtel It depends on the strategy: for deeptech or highly technical funds, yes. But many great companies are about execution. A mix is best. Technical people can get lost in the product and miss the rest; he isn't technical himself, and a different view makes the team better.

How competitive is growth? Do you fight American funds?

CompetitionAI

Nicolas Herschtel The faster a company grows, the more competitive it gets. On AI companies, US funds deploy capital at valuations IRIS won't match, so IRIS isn't the best fit there. For other strong companies, you have to be chosen by the founders.

What's your secret to winning a competitive deal?

Winning

Nicolas Herschtel No secret: a lot of work. Prepare every meeting, work with all the stakeholders, shareholders and advisers, provide references. He can't rely on the brand alone, since other funds are strong too. And respect the founder's time: a six-week process takes them away from running the company.

Have you ever pushed for more than a 1x non-participating preference?

Terms

Nicolas Herschtel He prefers creative structures, such as a mix of ordinary and preferred shares or a minimum return, over higher multiples. If a company accepts 2x participating, something is wrong: a bad process or desperate founders.

Don't aggressive preferences damage your relationship with the VCs who bring you deals?

TermsReputation

Nicolas Herschtel Yes. You might win the deal, but you need those relationships beyond it: you'll win once, not a second time. People trusting you matters more.

Given how rare billion-euro exits are in Europe, do you cap the valuations you pay?

ValuationExits

Nicolas Herschtel The reasoning is right. With 12–15 companies, you can't assume all of them reach €1B+. It isn't a hard cap, but he tends to invest at lower valuations, and goes higher only when he is really convinced a company can get there.

Why do you do this job, and what excites you?

Career

Nicolas Herschtel Working with companies and people. It's the most complete job there is, short of being an entrepreneur: marketing, finance, organisation. Investors are often "frustrated entrepreneurs", and he wanted to be one. Tech wasn't planned, but he loves how fast it moves and founders' energy to build something new.

What advice would you give someone our age starting their career?

CareersAI

Nicolas Herschtel AI is the challenge: it can already do much of what juniors did. Learn to leverage it (the hard skill) and build relationships (the soft skill AI won't replace). Do what you really love, because that energy is what makes the difference. Founders can get answers from AI in five minutes, so your questions have to add value beyond that. Be motivated and try to disrupt.
