Executive summary · 2 min 17 questions & answers
Executive summary2-minute read

Session 2 in six points.

  1. A fund is a pot of investors' money run by a regulated management company. Most are closed-end: about 10 years plus two one-year extensions, with 4–5 years to invest.
  2. VC is two jobs, not one: investing and raising money. Fundraising is half the work and takes 12–25 months for a first fund.
  3. LPs back a team: a track record, a clear thesis, a shared history and real alignment. GPs put around 2% of the fund in themselves.
  4. Returns follow a J-curve and a power law. Around 10% of a portfolio makes the fund, so every line must be able to become an outlier.
  5. The team should get rich on carry (about 20% of the gains, paid only after LPs are repaid), not on management fees.
  6. The fund regulations (LPA) are the contract: strategy, economics and governance, including key-person and GP-removal clauses.
A VC is two things: its reputation and its network. Daphni, on what a fund really sells, to founders and to LPs

02What a fund is

An envelope for other people's money.

A fund is a co-ownership envelope: it collects money from investors and invests it in startups. The vocabulary to know:

LPs

Limited partners

The investors who subscribe to the fund. They bring the money and carry the risk up to what they committed.

GP

General partner

The team that manages the fund through a management company. Its job: manage the portfolio on behalf of third parties.

On the fund's balance sheet, the liabilities are the LPs' subscriptions and the assets are the stakes it takes in startups. Most funds are closed-end: they live about 10 years, usually with two one-year extensions written into the contract.

The third actor: the depositary

Next to the management company sits the depositary, necessarily a credit institution. It holds the fund's assets and acts as a guarantor for subscribers. A startup stake is just a line in a company's share register, so an independent third party checks that the fund really holds what it says it holds.

03French vehicles & actors

Three legal wrappers, one structure.

In France, a fund takes one of three main forms. Some have a legal personality, others don't and must be represented by a management company approved by the AMF, the French markets regulator.

FCPR

What it is

Fonds commun de placement à risque. The generic venture fund. No legal personality, so it is always represented by an AMF-approved management company, and the fund itself is approved by the AMF.

For whom

A broad investor base, including retail products such as insurance-linked savings where FCPRs serve as underlying units.

FPCI

What it is

Fonds professionnel de capital investissement. A professional fund that doesn't need prior AMF approval, with more freedom in its rules.

For whom

Sophisticated investors only: people and institutions wealthy enough that a risky investment won't put them in danger.

SLP

What it is

Société de libre partenariat. A limited partnership with a legal personality, structured like a company limited by shares, close to the Anglo-Saxon LP model.

For whom

Professional investors, often international LPs who recognise the partnership format.

04LPs & why they invest

Who puts money into a fund.

The LP base in Europe looks very different from the US. Six typical profiles:

1
Public investors

Bpifrance in France, the EIF at European level, and "Bpifrance-like" agencies in other countries. Around 25–30% of a French VC fund often comes from Bpifrance. Essential while the ecosystem matures, but the asset class has to prove it can return money on its own.

2
Asset managers

Insurers, banks and mutual groups. They keep about 90% of their assets liquid to pay claims, and can put a small slice in private assets like VC.

3
Funds of funds

Increasingly present. They access the best funds and allocate according to their own thesis, and bring co-investment opportunities.

4
Corporates

A financial return, but above all access to innovation. An early-stage fund sees trends well before they do, shares its analysis on a market or technology, and sends startups worth testing: an open-innovation hub inside the fund.

5
Family offices

Single-family or multi-family. Often invest directly in startups too, so they work closely with the fund, share analysis and open their networks, since many of these families built industrial groups.

6
Pension funds

The number one LP type in the US. In France and most of Europe they are largely missing, and the speakers would like to see that change.

VC is a small, risky asset class, riskier than private equity or LBOs. Part of the job is acculturation: explaining to LPs that returns take longer but can be more interesting.

Fun fact · Corporate LPs

For many corporates, a VC fund is an innovation radar as much as an investment.

Because early-stage funds see startups years before large groups do, corporate LPs use them to spot trends, get market analysis and meet startups to pilot, things their own money can't buy directly.

05Fund economics

How a fund makes money.

In the first years a fund invests and pays management fees, while young startups haven't yet been revalued. On paper, LPs seem to lose money. As companies grow, raise again and get sold, value appears. This is the J-curve.

The two numbers LPs ask for

TVPI

Paper performance

Total value to paid-in: is the portfolio, as valued today, worth more than the money invested?

DPI

Real money back

Distributions to paid-in: how much cash has actually been returned to LPs so far?

Fees

Management fees

Paid every year by the fund to the management company, on the commitments or the invested amount. They keep the firm running. Some fund expenses (transaction or legal fees on deals) are also charged to the fund.

~20%

Carried interest

The team's share of the gains, paid only if the fund performs. What should motivate the team, more than salaries or nice offices.

The waterfall, step by step

1
Return the capital

LPs get 100% of the money they put in back first.

2
Hurdle

LPs then receive a minimum return. Like a liquidation preference, it isn't guaranteed: if the fund doesn't generate enough, there is nothing to pay.

3
Catch-up

The team catches up so that, overall, the gains are back in an 80/20 split (in practice 25% of what LPs received above their capital).

4
80 / 20

Every further euro of gain is split 80% to LPs and 20% to the team.

Worked exampleA €100M fund that returns 2x makes €100M of gains. The team shares €20M. For a team of about ten people, that is a meaningful amount, and the reason to maximise carry rather than fees.

Carried shares & vesting

The team invests like LPs through ordinary A shares, and buys B shares (carried interest shares) that entitle it to the 20%. For funds investing in SMEs, French tax rules require the team to invest at least 0.25% of the fund in carried shares: a small amount that can turn into significant gains. B shares vest, much like a startup employee's BSPCE but stricter: leave after two years and a cliff can mean you get nothing; leave in year 7 and you might keep about 70%. It is a strong incentive to stay the whole life of the fund.

06Portfolio math

Ten percent makes the fund.

A portfolio of 30–40 companies doesn't perform evenly. Roughly, many fail, about 40% return more or less what was invested, and around 10%, the outliers, generate the real performance.

The 10%IllustrativeWrite-offs

The session's example, on a €100M fund aiming for about 3x net: Daphni targets around 15% ownership when it invests. Startups raise again and the fund gets diluted to about 10% at exit. To return roughly €300M+ with 10% stakes, the portfolio must be worth around €3–3.5B collectively at exit. With only 10% of companies driving that, each investment has to be able to become a very large company.

Not every fund plays this game. Some have a less risky strategy and don't chase outliers as hard. That choice is part of the thesis sold to LPs.

07What a GP sells to LPs

LPs don't buy deals. They buy a team.

When raising, a fund is pitching itself. The five things LPs look at:

1
Track recordTVPI · DPI
What LPs check

Investments already made, as a fund or as individuals for a first fund. Are they well valued on paper? Has money actually come back?

Why it matters

Emerging managers without a track record struggle the most. Funds grow step by step: prove you can deploy €20M, then €80M, then €150M.

2
Thesis & complementarityWhy you, why now
What LPs check

A clear thesis for the next five years and a portfolio built around it. Example: in deeptech, a fund that can access research labs and independent experts can take strong bets on high-value projects.

Why it matters

LPs build their own portfolio of funds. Yours must bring something the others don't. Reputation and network open doors, to founders and to LPs.

3
Shared historyTeam risk
What LPs check

Has the team worked together before, and well? A diverse team also helps, especially as innovation now comes from everywhere.

Why it matters

Partner break-ups are one of the top reasons funds fail. LPs increasingly ask for a shared history before backing a first-time team.

4
AlignmentGP commitment ~2%
What LPs check

LPs only invest if the team puts its own money in: at least about 2% of the total raised.

Why it matters

On €100–300M funds this is a large personal amount, and it grows with each fund. Shared risk is the point.

5
Stability10 to 14 years
What LPs check

Who the key people are and how the firm will be run over the fund's life, which in France can stretch to 14 years.

Why it matters

LPs invest for a decade. They need to believe the same team will be there to finish the job.

08Investment thesis

Four choices that define a fund.

1
Stage & ticket

Which maturity, what average ticket, and so how many companies for a given fund size. How much is kept in reserve for follow-ons (at Daphni, roughly €1 kept for every €1 invested at first, though not every company gets it: some die quickly).

2
Portfolio construction

How many lines: 30–40 investments to spread risk while maximising the chances of finding outliers. Many small tickets cost more per line in time and diligence.

3
Sector focus

Concentration means more risk but deep industry knowledge and a better read of the issues. Diversification spreads the bets.

4
Geography

France, a region, Europe or the world? LPs ask you to justify that you can do deals in Poland, the US or Brazil. Public money can also restrict where it is deployed.

Lead

Bigger ownership, more time per deal, a board seat and more influence on the company's success.

Follow

Trust another fund's thesis and conviction, spend less time on diligence, write more tickets, reach slightly more mature companies.

09Fund strategies & models

Six ways to run capital.

Different structures suit different strategies. The session focused on the closed-end fund, by far the most common.

Closed-end fund

How it works

Fixed life (about 10 years), money raised up to a final close, then no new subscriptions. Capital is called as needed and returned as companies are sold.

Fits

Most VC firms, Daphni included. What institutional LPs know best.

Evergreen

How it works

No fixed term. Stays open, accepts new subscriptions and can buy back existing investors, offering entry and exit windows.

Fits

Patient strategies that want to hold companies until a good exit, at the cost of offering liquidity.

Multi-fund platform

How it works

One management company running several strategies side by side, each in its own vehicle.

Fits

Established firms. Daphni runs a generalist, a consumption and a diversity fund.

Fund of funds

How it works

Invests in other funds rather than in startups, following its own thesis.

Fits

LPs who want diversified access to the best managers.

Solo GP & micro fund

How it works

A small fund around one person, often a successful founder or operator.

Fits

Investors with a strong personal network, often starting with friends and fellow founders as LPs.

Deal-by-deal SPV

How it works

A special purpose vehicle raised for a single investment.

Fits

Testing a thesis or building a track record before raising a full fund.

Secondaries: when an LP wants out early

An LP can sell its stake to another investor on the secondary market. Daphni doesn't blacklist sellers: a new LP entering the base is a new relationship. What to watch is the discount. If an LP is ready to sell at 50% below your reported value, that is a bad signal about your portfolio, and everyone rushing out at once is worse. Specialised secondary funds now offer liquidity after 8, 10 or 12 years. At Daphni the rate is very low: two investors left the first fund.

10Life of a closed-end fund

Ten years, five phases.

1
Commitments & first close

LPs commit an amount (say €100,000) and must answer each capital call. Once enough is committed, the fund holds a first close and is created. Later closes follow until the final close; after that, no new money comes in.

2
Investment period

About 4–5 years to make first investments, so companies have the rest of the decade to create value. Capital is called step by step, not all on day one.

3
Follow-ons

Reserves support the best companies in later rounds.

4
Exits & distributions

As companies are sold, proceeds go back to LPs along the way, not all at the end.

5
Liquidation

At about 10 years the fund should be liquidated. In practice it uses its contractual extensions, usually two of one year each, and must be fully wound down after that.

Defaulting LPs

Failing to answer a capital call is heavily penalised. The defaulting LP's stake can be bought back at a steep discount (the example given was half its value), and it loses its rights to the rest.

Sizing the fund

Fund size follows the team's real investment capacity: how much it can deploy per year, with how many lines and reserves. Firms then grow fund by fund. In practice many target more than they manage to collect, because raising is hard.

11Key terms of the LPA

The fund regulations are the contract.

Everything promised to subscribers is written into the fund regulations (the LPA, or règlement du fonds). By subscribing, each LP accepts all of it. It is drafted so tightly that there is no room to play between the lines.

Strategy & size

Covers

Investment policy, target size and hard cap, term, diversification ratios and exclusions.

In practice

It is a financial product: if a team suddenly put everything in Bitcoin, LPs would be right to change the manager.

Economics

Covers

Management fees (base and rate), fund expenses, carried interest and hurdle, the distribution rules between A and B shares.

In practice

Negotiated with the largest LPs. The others accept the result.

Governance

Covers

Key-person clause, exclusivity, successor-fund rules, the advisory committee and removal of the manager.

In practice

LPs back a team. These clauses make sure it stays the team they backed.

1
Key person

If one or two named partners leave or stop dedicating their time, investment is suspended until a solution is approved by LPs, typically a replacement with an equally strong reputation. Then the investment period can reopen.

2
Successor fund

No raising a new fund on the same strategy while the current one is still investing, or the two products compete. Once enough is deployed, you can raise the next one, and the previous fund stops making new investments. Two generations can't invest in each other's companies (Blue couldn't invest alongside Purple).

3
Advisory committee

A fund has no board, so the LPA recreates one: the most influential LPs (Bpifrance and the main institutions) sit on it and are consulted on structural decisions, conflicts of interest and authorisations.

4
Removing the manager

A qualified majority of LPs can replace the management company. The majority needed is lower in case of fault than in a "no-fault divorce".

5
Side letters

Specific requests from individual LPs outside the main framework, for example a monthly deal-flow meeting on their topic.

12Fundraising

GPs are founders too.

VC communication is all about investing. But a firm has two pillars, and the first one is raising money. It is the whole team's job, and raising a fund is harder than raising for a startup: there are far more people to convince.

No fundraise, no firm. Paul Bazin & Étienne Renoux, on the first pillar of a VC firm
  • Same motion as a startup round. Pitch, data room, due diligence, negotiation.
  • Sales discipline. A CRM, a pipeline, dozens of meetings for each LP that commits. Around 80 LPs in one vehicle, with a low success rate.
  • Long cycles. 12 to 25 months, longer for a first-time fund. Pre-marketing starts about a year and a half before the first close.
  • One window. After the final close, no new money comes in.
  • Data-hungry LPs. Asset managers have teams to crunch data, so the data room must be strong: history, thesis, deal flow, fund regulations.
  • Warm intros only. Cold LinkedIn outreach barely works on institutional LPs, who talk to each other a lot. Relationships run in cycles: some LPs met during one fund only invest in a later one.
  • Be visible. Speak at events on topics where you have a real view. Saying things that aren't common makes people want to continue the conversation.
  • LP care. Keep LPs informed and looked after so they re-up, ideally with more than last time.
01–02

Thesis & market

Define the thesis, build the market study that supports it, prepare the data room.

03–04

First close & subsequent closes

Close once enough is committed, keep raising until the final close.

The consolidation wave

Many management companies were created in 2020–2021 by emerging managers, and the trend in Europe and the US is now consolidation. Small firms that can't raise again either become "zombies" (no new investments, living off fees on the existing portfolio, one or two people managing it on the side) or get bought by a bigger platform. Big platforms rarely buy them for the portfolio, which stays attributed to the individuals: they buy access to LPs. One acquisition mentioned involved a small fund whose founder brought an exceptional LP network.

13Inside a management company

Far more than an investment team.

1
Investment team

Sources and invests. The more senior you are, the more you also raise money from LPs.

2
Investor relations

The sales team of the fund: opens connections with new LPs, works out who could fit which strategy, keeps the link with existing investors.

3
Finance & fund ops

Capital calls, distributions, reporting, compliance with the many rules, and the quarterly valuation of every holding.

4
Legal

Compliance, and structuring each term sheet, including liquidation preferences. A preference lets the fund recover its investment first, but like the hurdle it isn't guaranteed: if the company's value can't cover it, there is nothing to get.

5
ESG & mission

Makes sure Daphni respects its mission-company commitments and the impact criteria in its funds.

6
Platform, community & marketing

Builds the network, multiplies connections and automates what can be automated, so the team spends its time where it adds value: talking to founders and helping the portfolio.

How the portfolio is valued each quarter

Every quarter, the finance and investment teams give each holding a value, following valuation rules and checked by auditors. New funding rounds set the reference. Until 2022, cheap money pushed valuations up; when rates rose sharply in late 2022–2023, valuations collapsed. If a company is valued high but doesn't need cash, it can stay at the entry level. If it needs cash, the value must reflect the risk of a down round. And when an investor offered roughly double the last round's valuation for a portfolio company months later (the example was Back Market), Daphni didn't simply mark up to the headline: the team agreed on the value it could actually justify.

Key takeawayAn emerging manager has to cover all of these jobs from day one, usually with a handful of people. That overhead is part of why consolidation is happening.

14 · Q&A with the Baby VC batch #14 fellows17 questions

Questions & answers.

Is an LP who resells on the secondary market blacklisted by the fund?

Secondaries

Daphni No, quite the opposite. A new LP entering the base is a new relationship, maybe someone you'll pitch the next fund to. The seller gets liquidity to reinvest elsewhere. The thing to watch is the discount: a sale far below your reported value sends a bad signal. Asked how often it happens: very rarely so far. Two investors left Daphni's first fund, and specialised secondary funds are now emerging to offer liquidity after 8–12 years.

How do you decide the size of a fund?

Fund size

Daphni From the investment capacity you can credibly sell: tickets per year, number of lines, reserves. If the model works at €70M, you don't go beyond what the team can deploy. Then you grow with your track record and your LP relationships: firms typically start around €20M, prove themselves, then move to €80M and €150M. Emerging managers who can't justify their model tend to disappear within a few years.

Are the target size, strategy and lifetime written in the fund's statutes? Can LPs change manager?

LPAGovernance

Daphni Yes. Everything said to subscribers goes into the fund regulations, the contract with each LP. It is binding: it is a financial product, so if the team decided to put everything in Bitcoin, LPs would be right to replace the manager. Deployment pace is also framed: if after three years you have your 30 companies and the portfolio is doing well, you can stop new investments and start raising the successor fund, but not while you are still investing.

Do LPs all negotiate the terms with you?

LPA

Daphni No. You present the strategy and economics clearly to everyone, negotiate with the largest investors who put in their clauses, and the others accept the result when they sign their subscription. Specific requests that don't fit the framework go into side letters.

Do LPs in the same fund coordinate or help you?

LP relations

Daphni They are consulted through the advisory committee and can give useful tips, for example their view on a company founded by one of their former executives. They won't share confidential information. Beyond that it comes down to personal relationships: if you get on well with someone, they may give advice.

US-style back-to-back rounds at much higher valuations are spreading. How do you value them, and what do auditors say?

Valuation

Étienne Renoux Daphni invests in young companies that need time to mature, so value can't be proven within a week or a month, though there are exceptions. Valuations come from the following rounds, adjusted for the context: years of cheap money pushed prices up until 2022, then rate rises made them collapse. A company valued high that doesn't need cash can stay at its entry value; one that needs cash must be marked for the risk. When a much higher offer arrived only months after a round, the team didn't just mark up: Étienne Renoux and the investment team agreed on the value they could defend, and that is the exercise every quarter.

What is the role of juniors in fundraising?

JuniorsFundraising

Daphni Bigger than most juniors think, and often forgotten. Build the market study behind the thesis, the data room, the track-record documentation. Keep your ears open: anyone you meet could open a door to an LP. And do LP care throughout the fund: catch up with family offices, send them startups relevant to them. You learn things you'd never see from deals alone, and raising is half the job.

What was the tax incentive you mentioned?

TaxHistory

Daphni A French scheme, around 2017–2018, under which investing in these funds earned individuals a tax reduction on each euro invested. It made collecting money easier, but it pushed management companies to raise rather than to maximise performance.

Where does AI fit at Daphni today?

AIOperations

Paul Bazin It changed a lot. For ten years every deal file was rated by each member of the investment team. With generative AI, every incoming deal now gets a score with explanations: should we take the meeting, why, and which team member is best placed to understand it. On the fund side, capital calls customised for 80 LPs and reporting of around 100 pages per fund are automated. When a founder asks "do you know anyone at Intel?", the system finds who in the network has the latest interaction with that person. The aim: maximise the network effect, because a VC is its reputation and its network.

Why not combine a closed-end fund and an evergreen?

Fund models

Daphni Evergreen brings a lot of complexity. Because you offer liquidity, you can't get your valuations wrong, and you must have buyers on the other side when people want out. Institutional asset managers also prefer products they know: finite vehicles with an expected value over a set period.

On the 2% GP commitment: do partners take loans to fund it?

AlignmentPersonal

Paul Bazin An honest answer: his commitment grew over time. As a junior he had little to put in and wasn't offered much. On the latest generation the commitment was much larger, more than he had in cash, across several vehicles, and it means taking on debt. It works because the funds perform well. His view: when you are an investor, you invest personally too, and that is what maximises performance.

Beyond financial return, what does Time4 measure?

ImpactTime4

Daphni Time4 has a diversity thesis, and its criteria are written into the fund regulations: inclusion, the profiles of founders and teams, the projects and their mission against recognised standards. It is a long list, reported on regularly. Daphni as a firm also has its own mission commitments and objectives.

If AI changes junior roles, which skills matter now, for juniors and partners alike?

CareersAI

Paul Bazin The question every industry is asking. Those who will thrive have the soft skills and the passion for the job, and use AI to amplify it. Number one is curiosity: always wanting to learn, from founders and from sector analysis. Juniors aren't less valuable, their job is more exciting: no more producing endless documents, more spotting new markets with a fresh eye. Senior teams that have "seen 10,000 projects like that" can miss that the timing has changed; a junior who believes in it can catch it. In many funds, the best deals were sourced by juniors.

One LP or a diversified LP base: is it a strategic choice?

LPsStrategy

Paul Bazin Both work. With a single LP you skip fundraising, which is half the work, and that LP usually sits on the investment committee. But if it changes strategy, you're dead: "it's a bit like having a jockey on your back." An independent firm with a broad LP base is harder to build, but gives you the freedom to run your strategy, maximise performance and earn carry.

How do emerging managers raise without a track record?

Emerging managers

Paul Bazin Often they do have one, built inside another firm. Raphaël and Jérémy left Alven to found Singular after contributing heavily to Alven IV, one of France's best-performing funds: they had created that value and launched their own firm. It is harder for founders who say "I'll launch the fund I wish I'd had": it takes a very big entrepreneurial success to justify managing other people's money. They usually start with their own circle, put in a larger GP share to align even more, and bring in successful founder friends as first LPs.

So you built your career in investment rather than as a founder?

Career

Paul Bazin He arrived saying he didn't want to do investment at all. An engineer by training, he went to business school to learn how to value and sell a product, and thought the next step was founding. What he realised is that every company story is different, with signals that lower or raise the risk but never decide it, and that he loves learning all day, whereas a founder has to stay focused. He still might start something one day if the right idea comes, but he is happy where he is.

Is regulation a moat, or a brake on European startups?

RegulationEurope

Paul Bazin Mostly the opposite of a moat in Europe: regulation fragments the market. A US startup addresses 350 million people from day one; a French one starts with 70 million and different rules in labour, finance and healthcare. He hoped GDPR would be a moat for European companies, and it could have been. Mastering a regulation can let you move faster once inside a regulated market. But a direct competitor in a less regulated market can grow faster, raise more and then invest more than you in your own market.
