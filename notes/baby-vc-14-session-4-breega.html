Early-stage due diligence with Breega.

Executive summary · 2 min The 8-pillar scorecard
Executive summary2-minute read

Session 4 in six points.

  1. Breega looks for five things: a top team on the topic, an acute pain with a clear "why now", tangible defensibility, traction that proves willingness to pay, and a model that can return the fund.
  2. From first call to term sheet takes 2 to 5 weeks: sourcing, a 30-minute discovery call, a 45-minute check call, the investment committee, then due diligence.
  3. Due diligence exists to dig into the committee's doubts and confirm or kill the thesis, by crossing three sources: founders, the ecosystem and documents.
  4. The deliverable is a scorecard in eight pillars: team, reference calls, competition, market, business plan, unit economics, exit and cap table, ending in a go / no-go.
  5. Never take the founders' numbers as given: rebuild the market size and the business plan yourself, then compare.
  6. Expectations depend on stage: at pre-seed most pillars are a bet; by Series A+ each needs proof.

02The thesis

Five things Breega looks for.

1
A top team on the topic

Rare expertise tied to the problem, complemented by a business and execution profile.

2
An acute pain and a clear "why now"

The cost of doing nothing must be obvious, not a "nice to have".

3
Tangible defensibility

A technical or go-to-market moat, not just technology anyone can copy.

4
Traction that proves willingness to pay

Paid, renewed pilots and growing ARR, not just letters of intent.

5
A scalable, "fund returner" model

The implicit question at every discovery call: can this model generate a VC return?

03From first call to term sheet

Six steps, 2 to 5 weeks.

1
Sourcing

A qualified lead, tracked in the CRM.

2
Disco

A first 30-minute discovery call with the founders. Then an internal debrief.

3
Check

A second, 45-minute call with another partner and an associate. Then another internal debrief.

4
Jedi Council

The investment committee.

5
Exploration

Due diligence: customers, experts, competitors.

6
Term sheet

Closing the deal.

2 to 5 weeksbetween the first call and the term sheet.

04Due diligence

Check the thesis before investing.

Due diligence is what happens between the investment committee's "go" and the term sheet.

Why & how

Why

Dig into the committee's doubts to validate or invalidate the thesis.

How

Cross three sources: the founders, the ecosystem (customers, experts, competitors) and the documents (data room, cap table).

Who & how long

Who

The deal team: a partner, an associate who sets the work plan, and an analyst.

How long

1 to 3 weeks. 3 to 4 days if a competing term sheet is on the table.

Use case · Wealthcome

The problem: wealth managers juggle scattered tools and a growing regulatory burden. The solution: an all-in-one platform covering aggregation, compliance, CRM and simulations. Customers: independent financial advisers, private banks and insurers. Team: three founders from the industry, since 2022.

Questions left after the committee: the founders' dynamic and ability to scale; the real depth of the tech and AI layer; the competitive landscape once the historical leader weakens; realistic market share in France and potential abroad; the ability to serve large accounts. Each pillar below answers them.

05Pillar 1 · Team & execution

The right team to build a €1B startup?

Each founder is rated on seven criteria, with a traffic-light score from green to red.

The seven criteria

1 · Grit
2 · Resilience
3 · Learn fast & decide quickly
4 · Results-oriented
5 · Pragmatically project the organisation
6 · Attract & retain top talent
7 · Trust

Questions to ask

"What if your co-founder left?" tests resilience.
"Our cheque arrives tomorrow: what do you do?" tests how they project the organisation.
"Will you come to us with bad news?" tests trust.

Founders' dynamic: the DISC model

To understand the person behind the founder, Breega runs a session with a coach from its Scaling Team, based on the DISC questionnaire, then debriefs the founders. It helps founders step back as leaders, helps Breega anticipate how the relationship will hold in hard times, and matters for performance: the dynamic between founders shapes how the company scales.

Dominance

Tasks · extravert

Immediate results, action, love of a challenge.

Influence

People · extravert

Enthusiasm, collaboration, recognition.

Conscientiousness

Tasks · introvert

Accuracy, quality, analysis.

Steadiness

People · introvert

Support, stability, cooperation.

Wealthcome

A trio from the industry with strong founder-market fit, all with profiles very oriented towards results and speed. The DISC session led to concrete recommendations to structure the leadership team for the long term. Point to watch: hiring senior profiles to scale.

06Pillar 2 · Reference calls

Do people who know the market confirm the story?

Who to call

1 · Customers and prospects, suggested by the startup and found independently
2 · Sector experts: senior operators, researchers
3 · Competitors, through their investors
4 · Investors: existing ones and sector funds
5 · Team references: former employers and colleagues

In practice

Start from the risks in the business plan. Source widely: about 50 profiles for roughly a 15% response rate. Run the calls. Extract the insights.

Wealthcome

Investors, experts, customers and business angels confirmed a real appetite for an alternative to the historical leader. The most-cited risk: missing the AI shift.

07Pillar 3 · Competition

Who else solves this, and how do you win?

Three principles

1 · Look beyond the competitors the founders name.
2 · Compare the money raised by competitors and adjacent players: are we too late?
3 · The real question: what is the moat?

Six moats

Network effects · Intellectual property · Data · Switching costs · Economies of scale · Brand

How: start from the founders' deck, complete it with research tools and databases, then validate with the reference calls.

Wealthcome

Dozens of players mapped by segment: incumbents, all-in-one challengers, niche tools and data layers. Result: one dominant but weakened historical leader, and several "competitors" that turned out to be potential customers.

08Pillar 4 · Market

Would 1% of the market be €100M of revenue?

Top-down

Start wide, then narrow

Market size × % that is the target × % in the geography × % truly accessible.

Bottom-up

Start from the field

Number of potential customers × annual contract value (ACV) = potential revenue.

Never reuse the founders' figuresAlways recalculate the market yourself from independent sources, then compare with theirs. Ideally, the top-down and bottom-up estimates converge.

Why now? Four typical triggers

1
A regulatory change

A new rule creates an obligation, and so demand. Example: mandatory e-invoicing.

2
A technological breakthrough

A new technical building block unlocks new uses. Example: LLMs.

3
A behavioural shift

Customers change their habits. Example: widespread remote work after 2020.

4
New infrastructure

What now exists makes possible what wasn't. Example: the cloud, removing the need for your own servers.

Wealthcome

The market, recalculated bottom-up from software spending in the target countries, meets Breega's threshold. The "why now": AI can shift part of the middle- and back-office payroll to software. Point to watch: France alone isn't enough, it needs Europe.

09Pillar 5 · Business plan

What has to be true for this to work?

Five areas to challenge

1 · Market and growth
2 · Unit economics
3 · Costs and hiring
4 · Cash, runway and milestones
5 · Key assumptions

In practice

Founders' BP: history, projections, assumptions and formulas; if there isn't one, rebuild it.
Stress-test: challenge the five areas and test the key assumptions.
Deal team BP: Breega's own version of the plan.

Wealthcome

A plan built by segment. Growth relies on large accounts, which are signing faster than planned. Point to watch: the pace of deployments.

10Pillar 6 · Unit economics

Profitable and scalable in the long run?

Calculating LTV / CAC

1
Customer lifespan

How long a customer stays, measured by cohorts (1 ÷ churn overestimates it).

×
Contribution margin

Revenue per customer minus variable costs, excluding fixed costs.

=
LTV

What a customer brings in over their whole lifetime.

÷
CAC

Marketing, sales and onboarding costs ÷ new customers over the period.

=
LTV / CAC

Benchmark: about 3 to 1 in SaaS. Below, customers cost too much; above, you can accelerate.

Other useful ratios

  • CAC payback: months needed to recover the cost of acquiring a customer.
  • Gross margin: share of revenue left after the direct costs of service.
  • Magic number: new ARR generated for every €1 spent on sales and marketing.
  • Rule of 40: year-on-year growth + EBITDA margin should exceed 40%.
Wealthcome

Recurring SaaS where the bill grows with the customer. Healthy unit economics on small firms, a longer payback on large accounts. Point to watch: financial management.

11Pillars 7 & 8 · Exit and cap table

Who buys it, and is the cap table still motivating?

Exit: a fund's return logic

1
The goal

Return 3x the money to LPs in 10 years.

2
The reality

Out of 20 startups: 10 fail, 8 return 1 to 3x, and 1 or 2 pay back the whole fund.

3
The mindset

Every deal must be able to return the fund on its own. So look at how comparable companies exited, and how this one could.

Cap table: four things to check

1
Founders' incentive

Their remaining stake after the dilution still to come.

2
The option pool (BSPCE)

5 to 10% per round, granted or still to grant.

3
Existing investors

Their quality and their ability to follow in the next rounds.

4
Room for the new investor

Enough ownership, and room to reinvest.

In practice: ask for the cap table history since incorporation, talk to the other investors, and simulate the cap table over the next rounds.

Red flag

A cap table already unbalanced in the first rounds.

Wealthcome

Exit: two credible routes, acquisition by a wealth-management, insurance or software player, or an LBO once profitable, with precedents in Europe and the US. Cap table: founders still in the majority, so highly motivated; an existing investor wanting to reinvest beyond its pro-rata, a sign of confidence; and room for a new lead.
