Baby VC · Batch #14Session 3

Sourcing & screening with Hexa.

Detailed notes

With Samuel Boggio

Principal at Hexa, the Paris startup studio behind Aircall, Front and Spendesk.

Executive summary · 2 min 8 questions & answers
Executive summary2-minute read

Session 3 in six points.

  1. VC rests on five skills: sourcing, picking, winning, helping and exiting. Nobody is excellent at all five, so know where you are strong.
  2. Sourcing is a numbers game: roughly 1 in 100 companies seen becomes a deal, so the only lever is widening the top of the funnel.
  3. Most good deals come from your network and your outreach. Both compound, so start building them from day one.
  4. Edge comes from looking where others don't: unusual signals like exits, research papers and job postings, not the same "top school" filters as everyone else.
  5. Screening starts with fund fit (ticket, fund size, exit potential), then the problem, the "why now", the business model and the market.
  6. At early stage the team decides: founders who explain complex things simply, face reality and know their own problems.
The more you see at the top of the funnel, the luckier you get. Samuel Boggio, on why sourcing matters

01The speaker & Hexa

A sourcing machine, by trade.

Samuel Boggio calls sourcing "my DNA". His whole career has been built on finding companies before others do.

Samuel Boggio, Principal

He started with internships in VC, including Alven in 2021. The setup there was unusual: he was the only analyst, with no associate, covering all of Europe for a fund aiming to back the best companies at Series A. He describes being overwhelmed for six months, but it shaped him. His first full-time role came at a fund preparing its launch, where he helped build a sourcing machine from scratch. He joined Hexa about two and a half years ago.

Hexa

Hexa (called eFounders until 2022) is one of Europe's largest startup studios, founded in 2011. It creates companies from its own ideas, recruits founders to run them and takes a significant stake. Its best-known creations include Aircall, now a global business with hundreds of millions in ARR, Front and Spendesk.

Samuel Boggio works at Hexa Scale, its majority-investment arm. It takes control of B2B software companies that haven't followed a hypergrowth path and helps their founders scale, working operationally inside the companies it backs. A recent example is Filiz, a software platform for schools.

02The job

Five skills. Pick your battles.

Succeeding in venture rests on five abilities. No investor is excellent at all of them, and Samuel Boggio rated himself openly on each.

1
Source

Find the founders and companies, ideally very early. His strongest skill. Today's session.

2
Pick

Decide whether it makes sense to go deeper and enter due diligence. "Good", in his words. Today's session too.

3
Win

Win a competitive process, so the founder says "I want this person on my board." Where he says he still has to progress.

4
Help

Support founders. Comes with experience: the more situations you have seen, the better you adapt.

5
Exit

Sell the company. Either you've built and sold one yourself, or you learn through the sale processes of companies you sat on the board of.

03Why sourcing matters

An option game, at every step.

VC is "quite mathematical, quite stupid and mean": the more companies you see at the top of the funnel, the higher the chance you find the one. At every stage, the odds that someone says "we stop here" are very high.

Key takeawayConversion barely moves. To do more great deals, widen the top: every extra 100 companies seen yields roughly one more deal.

For an analyst, that means meeting founders all week knowing only about 15% will make it to a first meeting, and around 3% to a second meeting with a director or partner. The more you send up, the higher the chance that at least one reaches the end.

This founder, I found him. Through me, we got access to this deal. Nothing beats that feeling. Samuel Boggio, on why he loves sourcing

As an intern at Alven, his goal was to push one company all the way through. The fund didn't invest in the end, but getting it that far was his measure of success.

04Power law

One deal can make the whole fund.

In a portfolio of 10 companies, a rough rule: 6 or 7 return 0 to 1x (and 1x after ten years is worse than a savings account), 1 or 2 return 3 to 5x, and with luck 1 or 2 return 10x or more. Every fund is hunting for that fund returner.

10x+Illustrative0–1x
Benchmark & Uber

Benchmark put about $12M into Uber's Series A, from a fund of roughly $400M. By Uber's IPO that stake was worth around $7B. One bet returned the fund many times over, enriching the whole firm, "even if they had done nothing else".

It's also why, in his words, VC is not a job where you get rich on salary: the reward comes from returns.

05Where deals come from

Four channels, two that matter most.

1
Network

Former colleagues, peers, people you worked with, your portfolio founders. In the broadest sense, and the biggest source.

2
Outbound

You go and find the founders yourself, systematically or around a thesis.

3
Other investors

A fund asks you to complete a round, or, as at Hexa Scale, a founder isn't ready to sell a majority and asks for intros to the funds you work with.

4
Inbound & portfolio

Founders who reach out, and people leaving your portfolio companies to build new ones. It happens often.

The key figure: around 80% of deals come from either outbound or the network. The network is something you have to feed as early as possible.

Reputation decides

A founder picks a partner for at least three or four years, and with so much money in the market, you are rarely alone at the door. Reputation is what makes a founder choose you. It works both ways: when five or six founders independently say a fund was hard to work with, Samuel Boggio stops sending founders there, even without ever speaking to that fund. Benchmark is the opposite example: no team page, no big publications, yet everyone in the US wants them on their cap table.

06Building a network

Meet everyone, then go deep.

0–18

Months: meet everyone

In your first years, and even as an intern, have coffee with as many people as possible. Some of it will feel like a chore. You have to go through it.

18+

Months: go deep

Focus on the people you genuinely enjoy and build relationships strong enough that they become friends.

Deal-flow sharing is a trust game. With someone you don't know, you share small talk, never the top founder you met last week. Once you trust three or four peers and share things that are genuinely useful, you get the best possible coverage of your geography. Break that trust and you're out.

Give before you takeMeet a founder who isn't a fit? Send a WhatsApp to a peer at the right fund and make the intro. You did the work for them, they take the call, everyone's happy, and it comes back.

In practice it is a big part of the job: coffee chats, drinks, catch-ups. A partner he knows estimates that about a quarter of an associate's time goes to catching up with peers.

07Outreach

Find an edge others don't have.

Systematic

A machine: identify as many founders as possible who match the fund's thesis criteria, and contact them. Mostly LinkedIn plus a startup database.

Thesis-driven

Dig a market deep enough to find "a hole in the racket", then map every company in it and contact the best.

Databases let you filter by schools, previous employers, roles and headcount growth over the last six months. The trap: if you filter on top business schools, top engineering schools, ENS and 42, so does the fund next door. The day that founder goes into stealth, the lead lands in both inboxes at once and you have zero edge.

What it means, concretely

1
Define the target

Role, sector, company size, location. Example filters in LinkedIn Sales Navigator: Founder / CEO, software 11–50 people, France, headcount +20%, posted in the last 30 days.

2
Add signals

Hiring, growth, job changes: "headcount +35% in 6 months", "left a scale-up 4 months ago", "CEO of a stealth startup".

3
Save the list

Get new matches every week without redoing the search.

4
Reach out

A short, personal note.

Signals worth watching

1
Exits

When a company is acquired, employees with BSPCE (stock options) get cash, often after an earn-out of about 12 months. At a previous fund, he logged every exit: within roughly six months, many of those people start thinking about their own company.

2
Research papers

The best early-stage deeptech funds monitor new pre-print papers by keyword. A researcher anywhere publishes, the fund knows the same day. Almost nobody else does it.

3
Job postings

For companies at roughly $1–10M of revenue, headcount says little. He tracks job boards instead: when a company starts hiring finance roles (FP&A, CFO), it is usually growing fast and its back office is getting messy.

The payoff

Companies outside the usual radar: no funding news, no warm intro, found through company registries, hiring pages, niche directories and papers. You keep the judgement and the relationship.

A 2021–2022 model

Some funds ran a "copycat" playbook: pick a successful US company, decide a European version should exist, and recruit people still in their jobs to build it. In exchange for coaching and setup, the fund took around 30% of the company. Less common today.

08Brand & content

Be the obvious name on your topic.

Content doesn't mean flooding LinkedIn. The investors who do it well write to help founders, or people who could become founders tomorrow. Most early deals involve someone still in a job or in a grey zone: an idea, two people playing around. Helping them early is how you get the call later.

He gave three examples, without naming them here:

  • The specialist. An investor who left a top European fund to launch one of the first defense-focused funds, alone. Many expected her back quickly. She positioned herself early, publishes quality content on defense, and founders love working with her.
  • The connector. A director at a European fund who, before 30, is known by every team in Europe because she fed her network relentlessly.
  • The thesis builder. A principal at an early-stage fund who pushed her partnership into biotech from scratch, through initiatives that bring researchers and professionals together to create companies. The fund is now the first name people think of in that space.

Key takeawayPick subjects where you have real expertise and share it, internally and externally. Communities and events tied to your thesis count as much as posts.

09Making it a routine

Sourcing is a habit.

Sourcing is a habit, not a campaign. The one rule · a little every week beats a big push once a quarter
1
Be resourceful

Find a way in: a mutual contact, an event, a cold message that lands.

2
Be creative

Look where others don't: registries, papers, job boards, niche events.

3
Eat what you kill

Nobody hands you deal flow. What you source is what you get to work on.

4
Hustler mindset

Show up, follow up, move first. Speed and persistence win deals.

In practice

  • Block time. Fixed slots in your calendar (say Tuesday and Friday at 2pm). He once went a month without sourcing while working on four deals in parallel. Restarting was painful, and the longer you stop, the harder it gets.
  • Get referred. The best intro is a founder telling another: "Talk to Samuel, he helped me, he responds fast."
  • Write messages that stand out. Dozens of funds send the same automated note. Name the person, mention their company and what is at stake for them right now, and ask for a moment. That alone puts you ahead of most of the market.
  • Come prepared. If your message says you know their market, show it on the call. Founders who feel you are an expert want to keep talking.
"60% of the job is boring"

Interns often tell him after three or four months that sourcing feels repetitive. His answer: it's a meritocratic job. Bring back weak companies and you keep sourcing. Bring back a good one and you go deeper together: second call, expert calls, customer references, models. That's how the job gets exciting.

And if you believe in a company your team passes on, go back to your boss with your arguments: "for A, B, C, D, I think we should push."

10Screening · Fund fit

First question: does it fit the fund?

Sourcing and screening follow the thesis. The same company can be a yes for one fund and a no for another: each fund screens against its own strategy.

Stage & check size

A pre-seed fund doesn't assess traction the way a Series B fund does.

Sector conviction

Criteria follow from what the fund believes about a market.

Fund size

Each deal must be able to return the fund. The bar is different at €50M and at €1B.

In practice:

1
Ticket

If the fund has €5M left to deploy and the deal needs a bigger ticket, raise it proactively with your team rather than ignoring it.

2
Exit size vs fund size

A huge fund needs giant exits: with 10% ownership, a company must sell for a fortune to matter, and exits above $10B are rare outside the big AI labs.

3
Market too small

A €400M fund owning 10% of a company that can realistically sell for €300–400M gets €30–40M back. A great exit for the founders, but a poor allocation of capital for that fund.

Written for founders as a pitch template, it doubles as a screening checklist: each gap is a question to ask. Samuel Boggio's own questions, in more detail:

Problem & solution

Ask

What problem does it solve? Is it real and expensive for companies or people? Does the product actually answer it, and is there demand?

Watch for

Products looking for a problem.

Why now

Ask

Why build this today, not three years ago or three years from now? What has changed?

Watch for

With AI models moving this fast, the answer can change with every new release.

Competition

Ask

Who owns this market today, and how do you beat them? Is there a narrative that makes you win?

Watch for

Incumbents 20–40x your size. It takes time to analyse.

Business model

Ask

Can the company make money? What does a customer cost to acquire, and what does it bring in?

Watch for

Profitability matters again today; a few years ago it didn't.

Market size

Ask

Is it big enough for a fund returner? Historically over $1B; the bar is rising. Size it bottom-up (buyers × adoption × price) or top-down.

Watch for

Whether the market will grow: new uses, new products.

Market pull

Ask

Do customers come to you? When a market pulls, prospects who ignored you for years suddenly call.

Watch for

Operational friction: a company doubling in size fast struggles to deliver and hire.

Example · Filiz

Filiz aims to be the ERP of tomorrow for schools: one clean interface for students (grades, schedules, absences) and for staff (finance, admin, teaching teams). Incumbents exist and are large, but their products are dated, often not even SaaS. The narrative to win: a far better product against players 20 to 40 times bigger.

Markets are moving targets

Static view

How big is it today?

Current addressable market, incumbents' market shares, today's competitors.

Dynamic view

What will it become?

Growth rate and the "why now", expansion as price or usage changes, distribution channels opening or closing.

12Screening · The team

An excellent team makes the difference.

So many people build today that whatever you launch, someone is already working on it. On the same idea at the same moment, an average team and an excellent team end up in very different places.

Green flags

Co-founders who get along and complement each other. Clear roles. Explaining something very complicated so clearly you could re-explain it to your boss. Respect for reality: knowing their problems and anticipating the next ones. Resourcefulness. A clear, ambitious vision that brings in investors, employees and customers. Surrounding themselves with people better than them.

Red flags

Founders who don't know who should answer a question. Nobody clearly responsible for anything. Over-confidence that ignores reality: "it'll be simple, we'll make millions."

Rachleff's Law

When a great team meets a lousy market, market wins.

When a lousy team meets a great market, market wins.

When a great team meets a great market, something special happens.

Andy Rachleff (Benchmark co-founder), as quoted by Marc Andreessen, The Pmarca Guide to Startups, 2007

His favourite question: ask founders to explain the most complicated thing they have learned. The best ones leave you feeling you understood. The best founders also talk openly about what's hard: growing 10% in a week, but already worried that delivery won't keep up in three months, and asking for help to decide.

Repeat founders remain the strongest predictor: he shared data that around 40% of unicorn founders had founded a company before.

13Advice for juniors

How to stand out and get hired.

  • Go deep. Make sure you really understand what the company does. Have the intellectual honesty to admit when you don't: "I got it" too fast always comes back two weeks later.
  • Communicate clearly. If you found a great company but can't convey it clearly to your superiors, they won't spend 45 minutes on it.
  • Own your passes. Every investor has passed on companies that went on to do great. When a founder you passed on shows up again, you'll need good arguments for why.
  • Convert the last internship. Getting a first full-time contract is the hardest step: when he was replaced at Alven, the role drew around 150 applications. Aim for an offer, and if it can't happen, be good enough that your manager pushes you to the next fund.
  • Target the fund you dream of. Find a way to connect with its team.
14 · Q&A with the Baby VC batch #14 fellows8 questions

Questions & answers.

Do you dislike networking because it feels fake?

Network

Samuel Boggio It's not that it's fake. He'd rather sit down and analyse why a task took so long and how to do it better. But the network is unavoidable: meet everyone early, then invest in the few relationships that click. Trust works in circles: his close peers have their own two or three trusted people, and what matters is that what you share in confidence stays protected.

LinkedIn blocks scraping. How do you get around it?

Tools

Samuel Boggio Tools exist, but be careful with volume: stay at reasonable levels or you get flagged. He didn't go into detail. Worth knowing that LinkedIn's terms restrict automated scraping.

Is a founder's personal "trauma" behind the company a signal you look for?

Founders

Samuel Boggio Not really. The Filiz founders started from the idea that the French state runs many things badly. They found one it handles especially poorly, apprenticeship financing for schools, and their first product helped schools manage it. The CEO isn't an education crusader: he is a strong executor with a visionary product. Someone who worked at a big competitor and decides to build the next generation is another strong profile.

Do US founders sell the "we never lose, we'll touch the stars" story more?

Founders

Samuel Boggio He mostly talks to European founders. The really good ones know it's hard: they lay out their challenges and difficulties and come prepared to answer, because they need your help too. Don't dismiss a strong project just because the founder doesn't sell themselves well.

Repeat founders make up a large share of unicorns. How hard is it to back one who failed before?

Repeat founders

Samuel Boggio He's comfortable with it. A founder who failed has learned a lot, often through very hard times, and usually carries a strong desire for revenge. Both of his partners fit that profile: one sold his previous company for far less than he hoped, the other ran a mobility company through heavy debt. If a founder truly wants to change an industry, he can back them. You can still question how many hours they'll put in. Some investors disagree.

If you launched your own fund, what would the thesis be?

CareerThesis

Samuel Boggio Close to what he does today: majority stakes in software companies, potentially with more focus on succession and transmission.

Isn't that a search fund?

Structures

Samuel Boggio No. In a search fund, the searcher becomes the CEO and largest minority shareholder, backed by investors. At Hexa Scale he does few deals and doesn't run the company, but as the majority holder he has the governance to make changes, so if a deal fails, it's on him.

With the bar to build so low, have you ever tested a startup's moat by trying to rebuild its product?

MoatsAI

Samuel Boggio Some large investors say they can rebuild a product from scratch to test it. He'd love to see it: rebuilding an ERP is never a two-hour job, however strong the team.
