Baby VC · Batch #14Session 1

Inside early-stage VC with Kima Ventures.

Detailed notes

With Alexis Robert & Edouard Goût

General Partner and Investor at Kima Ventures, the venture arm of Xavier Niel.

Executive summary · 2 min 12 questions & answers
Executive summary2-minute read

Session 1 in six points.

  1. Startups win on speed of execution, not on cash.
  2. VC only makes sense for outlier outcomes: a few winners pay for the whole fund.
  3. A fund earns fees for raising money and carry for returning it. Know which game a fund plays.
  4. VC is a sales job: juniors source and qualify, partners close.
  5. Deal flow is shared. You win the hot deals by bringing value before asking for allocation.
  6. At seed, investing is recruiting: Kima weighs the team around 80%, the market 20%. Most mistakes come from loving the product while doubting the team.
You'll suffer, and you'll learn like never before. Alexis Robert, on why everyone should found a company once

01The speakers & Kima

Two founders turned investors.

Alexis Robert, General Partner

Alexis Robert describes himself as "a VC that knows how to rebuild a Linux kernel". He started coding as a child, grew up in open-source communities, and studied computer science and electrical engineering at Télécom Paris (2011–2015), followed by HEC Paris's specialized master in Management & New Technologies.

While still a student he was CTO of MyBee (now PayinTech), the first cashless payment provider for festivals. Before NFC smartphones existed, he built the payment terminals himself, hardware and software. The company raised $6.5M and was acquired by Merim Group.

He then co-founded ShowMango in London (2013–2015), backed by Seedcamp. The team shipped a new pivot roughly every two months for 18 months and nearly went personally bankrupt. The last version reached product–market fit, but the pivots had burned the cash. He applied to Kima with £2 in his bank account, joined in November 2015, and is now General Partner.

Edouard Goût, Investor

Edouard Goût sums himself up as "a VC with a Root-Me account". He trained at École 2600, a cybersecurity school, and worked as a penetration tester: an ethical hacker paid to find vulnerabilities in a company's software and report them. During his studies he co-founded a job-placement startup that helped schools track whether students were finding work-study contracts. It made about €4,000 of revenue in three years.

He then freelanced as a pentester, made €0 for ten months, and eventually built a client base of 15–20-person architecture firms in Paris. He wanted to build a company but lacked an idea and, by his own account, the people skills, so he moved into VC and joined Kima in early 2026. He still reads arXiv papers on weekends.

Kima Ventures

Kima is Xavier Niel's venture capital arm and one of the most active early-stage investors in the world. Mostly French founders, building tech anywhere in the world. Its portfolio includes Harmattan AI (pre-seed), Pennylane (seed), Pigment (seed), Sorare (co-led pre-seed), Alma (pre-seed), Agicap (seed) and Ledger (Series A). In H1 2026 the team added 97 startups, and 20VC's 2025 report ranked Kima #3 micro-VC in Europe and #1 in France.

02What a startup is

Speed of execution beats cash.

When Alexis Robert was founding, everyone asked him: "What if Google does the same thing?" His answer: Google has money, talent and the pick of recruits, but it is huge, political and slow. A small team that moves fast can win. OpenAI beating Google to market was his example.

Startups aim for massive, generational companies, which is why they chain Series A, B and C rounds. That ambition means risk and velocity, and it is not for everyone.

Startup vs tech SME

Many good tech SMEs exist: accounting software vendors, or a five-person company with a monopoly on software for Protestant places of worship. Their founders often earn more personally than startup founders. The difference is not the technology but the founder's ambition: do they want to revolutionize an industry and accept the danger and workload that come with it?

03Financing

Four ways to finance a company.

Equity

Definition

Selling shares of the company. Primary: new shares are issued and the cash goes to the company. Secondary: an existing shareholder sells shares to someone else.

Who provides it

VC funds like Kima, business angels, equity crowdfunding platforms, corporate VCs, family offices, and growth or private equity funds later on.

Debt

Definition

Borrowing money that is repaid with interest. Founders keep all their shares, but repayments are due whatever happens. Usually the right fit for profitable tech SMEs.

Who provides it

Banks, Bpifrance loans, venture debt funds and revenue-based financing providers.

Grants & subsidies

Definition

Non-dilutive public money for specific projects or sectors, often R&D and deeptech, and currently a lot of defense tech. Nothing to repay if the conditions are met.

Who provides it

Bpifrance, France 2030, the EU (EIC, Horizon Europe), regions, and innovation tax credits such as the CIR.

Self-financing

Definition

Bootstrapping: growing with the founders' own money and the company's revenue. Founders keep full ownership and control, but growth is limited by the cash available.

Who provides it

The founders' savings, early customers (revenue and prepayments), and sometimes friends and family.

VCs focus only on equity, and buying shares only pays off when the company becomes exceptional. In practice founders mix these sources. As Alexis Robert put it: "We're founders, not idiots. It's a cost-of-capital arbitrage."

Why shoot for the stars

Some push back: "You all want billion-euro companies, but there are very few in France. A €10M or €100M exit is great." Alexis Robert's answer: risk in startups is extreme. Co-founder conflicts, failure to find product–market fit, a new competitor (even an AI model shipping the same feature). Because so much can go wrong, you need a huge best case. Aim for the stars and you still land fairly high.

04LPs & smart money

Where the money comes from.

LPs (limited partners) are the investors who put money into a VC fund. The types covered in the session:

Funds of fundsGovernment agenciesCorporates & insurersFamily officesPrivate individualsAsset managersPension funds

Historically there were few funds and many companies looking for money, so funds set the terms. French tax incentives then pushed wealthy people to reinvest instead of paying tax, many new funds were born, and the balance flipped: today many funds compete for the best founders. Money alone is no longer enough, so funds sell smart money:

  • Reputation. Raising with a well-known fund puts you on stage and helps you recruit top talent.
  • The teacher effect. A VC sees one "class" after another at the same stage, so it can see your problems coming and help you avoid them, with intros to lawyers and experts.
  • Exit thinking. Some VCs ask from day one, "Who could buy your company?" and introduce potential acquirers. A sale process takes at least six months to a year.
  • Portfolio intros. Connecting complementary companies the fund has backed.
Fun fact · Government LPs

The CIA has a venture fund. Its pitch opens with "we are not the CIA's fund."

In-Q-Tel invests in startups on behalf of US intelligence agencies, one of the clearest examples of a government-backed investor. When Alexis Robert took a call with them, that disclaimer was the very first line of their presentation.

05Fund economics

How VCs make money.

A VC firm runs two entities. The fund holds the LPs' money and the shares it buys, and has no employees. The management company employs the team and invoices the fund for managing it. The split is a legal and tax question: you don't put staff inside a vehicle holding €100M of investors' money.

Alexis Robert's warning

You can live comfortably off fees: raise big funds, collect 2% a year, invest in "sausages", let partners take most of it. Some older French funds made their partners €10–50M this way, not through returns. Returns are hard: a Bpifrance fund-of-funds contact told him that matching a Livret A savings account already makes a Paris fund top tier, and many funds return 0.x times the money.

Why do LPs still invest? Diversification, keeping the ecosystem working (often at public request) and tax incentives. On the slide's sample of 5,000 companies, only a handful reached an IPO, which is why funds aim for unicorns.

06Power law

A few winners pay for everything.

VC returns follow a power law: a tiny number of companies generate almost all of a fund's gains, while most return little or nothing. The bars sketch a typical portfolio of 20 companies, sorted by what each returns.

Best outcomeIllustrativeWrite-offs

Return the fund is the test that follows: when investing, a VC asks whether this one company could, in its best case, pay back the entire fund on its own. If a €100M fund owns 1% of a company at exit, that company must sell for €10B to return the fund. A smaller fund or a bigger stake lowers the bar.

This is why Alexis Robert says to shoot for the stars, why a big fund must watch entry valuations more closely than Kima, and why Kima's small tickets let it back unusual founders: one Pennylane or Harmattan pays for many write-offs.

07The team

Who does what inside a fund.

Edouard Goût walked through the classic ladder. Titles and timelines vary a lot between funds, so the ranges below are typical, not rules. Small funds are much flatter: Kima invests around 100 times a year with a team of four.

1
AnalystIntern or 0–2 years
Day to day

Sources startups, takes first calls, builds market maps, screens decks and supports due diligence.

Decision power

Recommends and flags deals. No vote. The entry point most Baby VC fellows target.

2
Associate~1–3 years in the role
Day to day

Often joins after 2–3 years in banking, consulting or a startup. Qualifies deals, runs due diligence, writes investment memos and helps portfolio companies.

Decision power

Builds the case for a deal and presents it. No vote.

3
Senior Associate~2–4 years in VC
Day to day

Same work with more autonomy. Has seen enough deals to spot patterns, often specialises in a sector and starts shaping the fund's direction.

Decision power

Champions deals in front of the partners. Usually no vote.

4
PrincipalSometimes VP or Director · 5+ years
Day to day

Leads deals end to end and represents the fund with founders and other investors. Deals start coming to them directly, and they hand them down to the team. May sit on boards.

Decision power

Leads deals; the final call usually stays with the partners. On the partner track.

5
PartnerGeneral Partner (GP)
Day to day

Sets the strategy, leads the largest decisions, sits on boards, raises money from LPs and manages that relationship.

Decision power

Go / no-go at the investment committee. Shares in the carry.

VC is sales

VC is glamorized, but the job is finding deals, exactly like sales. Juniors work like SDRs: find leads (inbound, outbound, partnerships), take the first call, qualify against a grid, pass it on. Principals and partners work like account executives: put the final stamp on it, win access to the deal and close. Then comes the board and follow-on work.

08Sourcing

Deal flow is not proprietary.

People picture VCs on a throne waiting for pitches. In reality you are on WhatsApp with other funds all day, and everyone sees roughly the same companies. For the hottest deals, it is a fight.

A hot AI deal

A high-profile AI team raised a large round on a pitch deck alone. Alexis Robert spent almost two full days studying their model and finding relevant research papers just to get a first call. Three messages went unanswered. In the end Xavier Niel had to email the founder personally, Alexis Robert made three introductions and wrote to the lead investor. Kima's allocation was still cut from €150K to €100K.

Key takeawayWhen the front door is closed, find another one: an intro, a co-investor, real work on the founder's problem. Getting the call, then a place on the cap table, is the job.

Founders of hot companies are exhausted by inbound. One founder got hundreds of emails; some investors who couldn't reach him booked an appointment with his mother, a dentist, to ask her to get her son to reply.

The flip side: as in public markets, the alpha is often where nobody is looking. Alexis Robert cited Harmattan AI, which started with drones detecting landmines in farmers' fields and moved into defense. Few investors wanted it when Kima backed it in 2024; it has since raised a $200M Series B at a $1.4B valuation.

09Selection · Kima's thesis

Team first. Market second.

80%

The team

Learning speed, passion, drive and niaque (grit). People who will "blow everything up".

20%

The market

Big enough for a unicorn, because founders pivot around their initial market.

A pivot is changing what you build: a litter box for cats becomes a litter box for dogs. But you always pivot around your starting point. Alexis Robert's own events business went through many products and none worked, because the market itself had nothing to give. He sees HR tech the same way.

The hens analogy

If you sell teeth to hens and it doesn't work, you pivot to selling eggs to hens. But hens have no bank accounts. You'll keep pivoting around a market that can't pay. A good market lets a strong team find the right product, like a construction-site messaging startup Kima backed that can expand far beyond its first use case.

Why founders do it

A Baby VC fellow from batch #14 asked whether all founders aren't driven by definition. Alexis Robert: go to Station F at 10pm and see who is still there. If you are fully content, you join your friends at Accenture, buy a house and a dog. Many great founders have something unresolved that pushes them to take an irrational risk. Others start companies because "entrepreneur" sounds better at dinner than "transaction services at Mazars", and they tend to quit when it gets hard.

10Winning deals

Give value before you ask.

Once a fund wants a deal, it still has to be chosen. Treat it like a sales process: find out what the founder needs and deliver it before asking for anything. One founder needed customers in an applied-AI vertical; Kima found one or two in its network and made the intros. Favours done for co-investors also come back ("take Alexis in the round"). You get nothing for nothing.

Easy mode

Events, nice dinners, gastronomic restaurants, an influencer lifestyle. These VCs are gone within about five years.

Hard mode

Grinding and real work for founders, with long hours (Alexis Robert mentioned 8am to 1am for months). The ones who last and build a track record.

11Exits

The four ways out.

1
IPO

Listing on a stock exchange.

2
Acquisition

The company is bought by another company.

3
Secondary sale

Selling shares to another investor. Kima backed a 19-year-old at pre-seed, before he joined Y Combinator, followed every round from 2019, then sold its whole stake when the timing felt right.

4
Founder buyback

Founders who decide the VC path isn't for them buy investors back out once they can afford it, sometimes at 1x the original ticket.

12 · Q&A with the Baby VC batch #14 fellows12 questions

Questions & answers.

What separates a hot deal from a cold one?

Deal flow

Alexis Robert Hard to predict. Hot deals often come from a stellar CV or a known name mentioning the founder at a conference, then herd behaviour does the rest. A deal can be cold because the founder pitches poorly or the angle needs reframing (Harmattan: landmine-hunting drones vs drones that stop attack drones).

Example of an overlooked one: Cleavr, founded by a former Kima team member, reached meaningful revenue within six or seven months while other investors stayed away. Kima backed it from pre-seed; it has since raised an €8M seed round. Alexis Robert also noted that on the best deals, the investors who hesitated and put in less often ended up with the best returns; Kima wrote a small ticket in Ledger.

Beyond diversification, why write small tickets in so many companies?

Kima modelFounder support

Alexis Robert It lets Kima bet on people completely outside the bubble, which is where great companies come from. Examples: a founder who had spent ten years in prison; a 19-year-old; someone with a CV that "looks like nothing"; a founder carrying €2M of personal debt from a previous taxi-rental business, who saw the startup as his only way to ever repay it. A normal fund can't justify these politically. Kima can.

Kima's stance with founders is reactive, not intrusive: "When you need us, message us and we'll be there. Otherwise we won't bother you." Not seeing a founder for six months is fine.

The real value is in the low moments. A founder once called in tears, facing an employment tribunal, her round not closing, just back from her grandfather's funeral, ready to quit everything. The investor's job then is to be a coach and help her step back. Kima also connects founders with each other: you are the average of the people around you, and friends or family often can't relate to founder struggles.

How do you do due diligence properly in a few days, especially in fast-moving AI verticals?

Due diligence

Alexis Robert Separate the stages. At pre-seed and seed, companies have at most a few customers; investing is mainly a recruiting job. From Series A onward (next round roughly 10+ months later), then B and C, the numbers, legal diligence and market analysis carry more weight, especially with tickets in the millions.

There are always "40,000 reasons" not to do a deal, and the deeper you dig the more you bias yourself toward no. Diligence is about naming the uncertainties and deciding if you accept them ("maybe this founder will steal the money; I'm OK with that risk"). Strong founders often enter broken-looking markets knowing they'll adapt. Small tickets across many companies let Kima accept more risk.

What if the CEO, or a co-founder, is the problem?

Portfolio support

Alexis Robert The CEO role is mostly unglamorous: investors, board, PR and conferences, and recruiting, which is the hardest part. If a co-founder is weak, founders usually already know. Investors act through soft power: ask questions until the founder reaches the conclusion and owns it. Example: instead of saying "you need a sales playbook", ask "what do you think about building a playbook? How would that change this problem?" You never walk in and say the work is bad.

How do you stay current on AI, and judge whether a company's tech is solid?

Tech

Alexis Robert Following researchers directly on X rather than commentators, reading papers, podcasts, Hacker News and Y Combinator; he and Edouard Goût swap links on weekends. Founders also give the pulse.

On the tech itself: at the earliest stage, the team matters more. If the tech hits a wall, a strong team will spot it, change the approach or even the CTO, and keep going.

What is your biggest investment regret?

Mistakes

Edouard Goût Passing on an AI pentesting startup that raised about €4M a week before the session. Coming from pentesting, he asked how they handled the test authorization letter, the legal document every pentest starts with ("the Bible"). They said they didn't need it because they had insurance. To anyone from the field, a red flag. His lesson: he overfit on a market he knew and should have weighed the team's grit and execution speed more. Alexis Robert added that he still has doubts on that market: very crowded, and they are far from the leader.

Alexis Robert Passing at roughly a €5M valuation on a company now valued at billions. The pattern across Kima's mistakes: falling for the product or problem while finding the team average. The biggest wins came when the project looked shaky but the team was exceptional.

When raising a fund, how do you find LPs?

Fundraising

Alexis Robert Known institutions and funds of funds you can simply look up, referrals from other GPs who raised recently, family offices, and events such as SuperVenture in Berlin, Bits & Pretzels in Munich, and GP/LP dinners.

What happens to the remaining companies when a fund closes?

Fund lifecycle

Alexis Robert Good positions get sold or their proceeds returned to LPs (e.g. shares sold after an IPO). The hard part is the leftovers nobody wants, the "hot potato" at the end. Options: sell on the secondary market at a haircut (say a 20% discount) because you must close; extend the fund toward 15 years; move assets into a new vehicle; distribute the shares directly to LPs; hand them back to the founder; or offload a business that became an SME to a buyer. Kima, with a single LP, has no forced closing date.

Is backing unconventional founders specific to Kima because of Xavier Niel?

Kima model

Alexis Robert Yes, it is specific to Kima and its single-LP setup, and part of why he stays. In one case Kima was the only investor to say yes to a young founder and remained his only believer for three years.

Europe has a Series B/C "valley of death". Shouldn't funds just get bigger?

EcosystemEurope

Alexis Robert Don't believe every headline. When the press said there were no exits in France, Kima was having its best year ever: profitable for about a year and a half without needing money from Xavier Niel, so many exits the legal paperwork was hard to keep up with, and about €4M received on 1 October from three exits at roughly 10x. Large late rounds do happen (Mistral and others).

The real gap is the number of great companies. The post-Brexit story that France would be Europe's Silicon Valley (2020–2022) lost momentum around 2024, while Munich, London and Zurich came back. His advice: think cross-European. "Our neighbour isn't Lyon, it's Berlin." US investors fly anywhere for a great company; European investors need the same habit. He cited ElevenLabs (Polish founders) and Legora (Sweden, competing for the best US funds).

Does closeness to founders ever cloud judgment on valuation?

Valuation

Alexis Robert Sometimes Kima passes on price; he thinks Y Combinator companies are starting to look overpriced. But with exceptional founders, if you believe it can be worth a billion, a high entry still works.

Example: Pennylane at about €35M valuation, considered very expensive at the time. Kima couldn't reach the founder, so Alexis Robert had the analyst send his email. The reply: "I'm free in an hour." He cycled across Paris, arrived sweating, and closed €50K. Larger funds must be stricter on price, because each investment needs to be able to return the whole fund.

Kima focuses on France. Doesn't that limit the cross-European view?

Kima model

Alexis Robert The requirement is a French founder, not a French company. Kima has backed French founders in Sweden, Estonia, Pakistan and other unexpected places. Otherwise, the best French founders based abroad would be funded only by foreign investors.
